Additive manufacturing (AM) — building parts layer-by-layer from digital models — has evolved from prototyping technology to production-capable manufacturing for select applications. Production AM is most viable for: geometrically complex parts that are impossible or prohibitively expensive to machine; low-volume, high-value parts where tooling cost is prohibitive; customized parts (medical implants, dental prosthetics, consumer products); and spare parts with low demand but high criticality.

Metal AM processes include Selective Laser Melting (SLM), Electron Beam Melting (EBM), Direct Energy Deposition (DED), and Binder Jetting. Polymer AM processes include Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM), Selective Laser Sintering (SLS), Stereolithography (SLA), and Multi Jet Fusion (MJF). Material properties — tensile strength, fatigue resistance, surface finish — vary significantly by process and require extensive qualification before production use in regulated industries.

AM process control is significantly more complex than conventional machining: hundreds of parameters (laser power, scan speed, hatch spacing, layer thickness, inert gas flow, powder bed temperature) influence final part properties. In-process monitoring using pyrometers, melt pool cameras, and X-ray computed tomography (CT) enables defect detection during build, but closed-loop control of AM processes remains research-stage for most applications. Post-process inspection (CT scanning, metrology, destructive testing) is required for most aerospace and medical applications.

Regulatory qualification of AM parts for aerospace (FAA certification) and medical (FDA 510k/PMA) requires extensive testing: mechanical properties, fatigue life, corrosion resistance, dimensional accuracy, and biocompatibility (for medical). The qualification process typically takes 18–36 months and costs $500,000–$5,000,000, limiting AM adoption to applications where the performance or cost advantage is compelling. AM in aerospace has enabled parts consolidation (replacing 20-part assemblies with one printed part), weight reduction of 30–60%, and lead time reduction from 12 weeks to 3 days for complex brackets and heat exchangers.
